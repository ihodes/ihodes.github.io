The largest rotors in the Grand Coulee Dam weigh 2,000 tons each. The rushing waters of the Columbia River, gathered in Lake Roosevelt reservoir, spin these rotors, locked at 85.7 rotations per minute by the grid. Collectively, 21,000 tons of rotating steel spun by falling water provide 20 terawatt-hours of energy per year to the Western Interconnection.

The enormity of these as physical objects is difficult to comprehend. You might try to imagine the combined weight of 20 houses, including their poured concrete foundation, spinning rapidly. The enormity of the electricity produced is similarly monumental: generating the same with human labor would require 20 million people vigorously pedaling bicycles 24 hours a day, over the course of a year.

The rotors' rate of rotation is maintained by the rotation of all the other spinning masses connected to the grid. They are electromagnetically locked so that they produce a fixed 60 Hz alternating current. If they try to spin too slowly, the grid pushes power into them, speeding them up. If they try to spin faster, the grid pulls power from them, slowing them down. The rotors in the dam are connected by thousands of miles of aluminum line to the 200-ton steam turbines spinning at 1,800 rotations per minute in the desert of Arizona, at the Palo Verde nuclear plant, and millions of tons of rotating steel in other generators across the region.

When you turn on a light, the pull of that bulb creates a small drag on that rotor. No more than if you, ever so gently, brushed your finger across one of the rotors at the foot of Roosevelt Lake.
